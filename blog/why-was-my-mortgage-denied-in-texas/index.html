Why Was My Mortgage Denied in Texas? Common Reasons and What to Do Next

Getting a mortgage denial letter is frustrating, especially in today's competitive Texas housing market. Whether you're looking to buy in Katy, Houston, or elsewhere across the state, a mortgage denial can feel like a roadblock to homeownership. But here's the good news: understanding why your application was denied is the first step toward reapplying successfully.

As a Senior Loan Officer with CMG Home Loans serving the Katy and Houston area, I've helped countless borrowers work through mortgage denials and find pathways to approval. In this guide, we'll explore the most common reasons mortgages are denied in Texas and discuss actionable steps you can take to strengthen your next application.

The Top Reasons for Mortgage Denial in Texas

1. Credit Score Issues

Your credit score is one of the first things lenders evaluate. If your score is below the minimum requirement for your desired loan program, your application may be denied. Most conventional loans require a credit score of 620 or higher, though many borrowers find better terms with higher scores.

A low credit score typically signals to lenders that you've had difficulty managing credit in the past—late payments, high credit utilization, or collections accounts can all impact your score. If this is your barrier, consider spending 6 to 12 months improving your credit before reapplying.

2. Insufficient Income or Income Documentation

Lenders want to ensure you can afford your monthly mortgage payment. If your income is too low relative to the loan amount you're requesting, or if you can't adequately document your income, your application may be denied.

This is especially common for self-employed borrowers, freelancers, and gig economy workers in the Katy and Houston area who may have inconsistent income. Texas lenders typically require two years of tax returns and bank statements to verify self-employment income. Even W-2 employees can face denial if recent job changes or gaps in employment raise red flags.

3. High Debt-to-Income Ratio

Your debt-to-income ratio (DTI) compares your total monthly debt payments to your gross monthly income. Most lenders cap DTI at 43 to 50 percent, depending on the loan program and your overall financial profile.

If you're carrying significant credit card balances, car loans, student loans, or other obligations, your DTI may exceed acceptable thresholds. Paying down existing debt before reapplying can substantially improve your chances of approval.

4. Employment History and Job Changes

Lenders prefer to see stable employment history. Frequent job changes, gaps in employment, or recent career transitions can raise concerns about income stability. If you've changed jobs within the last two years, especially if there was a period of unemployment in between, lenders may request additional documentation or deny your application outright.

5. Insufficient Savings and Down Payment Funds

Many mortgage programs require borrowers to demonstrate liquid savings and a minimum down payment. If you're putting down less than 20 percent, you'll likely need private mortgage insurance, which adds to your monthly payment and must be factored into your DTI calculation.

Additionally, lenders want to see that your down payment comes from your own savings, not from borrowed funds. If the source of your down payment is unclear or appears to be a loan, your application may be denied.

6. Property-Related Issues

Sometimes the problem isn't with you—it's with the property. In the Katy and Houston markets, issues like low property appraisal, title defects, or concerns about the property's condition can lead to denial. If the home appraises for less than the purchase price, the lender may deny the application or require you to pay the difference out of pocket.

7. Recent Bankruptcy or Foreclosure

If you've experienced bankruptcy or foreclosure, you're not automatically disqualified from getting a mortgage. However, most lenders require a waiting period—typically three to seven years depending on the loan program and circumstances. A recent bankruptcy or foreclosure will significantly impact your eligibility.

8. Fraud or Misrepresentation on Your Application

This is a serious issue. If lenders discover inconsistencies between what you reported and what documentation shows, your application will be denied immediately. Always provide accurate, truthful information on your mortgage application.

What to Do After a Mortgage Denial

Request a Written Explanation

By law, lenders must provide you with a written explanation for the denial. Review this carefully to understand the specific reasons. This is your roadmap for improvement.

Check Your Credit Report

Obtain a free copy of your credit report from annualcreditreport.com. Look for errors or inaccuracies that might be dragging down your score. You can dispute errors directly with the credit bureaus.

Reassess Your Finances

Look honestly at your income, debts, and savings. If your DTI is too high, focus on paying down credit cards and other consumer debt. If your income documentation is weak, gather additional paperwork to strengthen future applications.

Specialized Loan Programs That Might Work for You

If traditional conventional loans aren't working out, there are alternative programs worth exploring:

  • FHA Loans – More flexible credit score requirements and lower down payments
  • VA Loans – For eligible veterans with favorable terms and no down payment requirement
  • USDA Loans – For rural borrowers with limited down payment options
  • Non-QM Programs – For borrowers with non-traditional income sources

Tyler can evaluate your specific situation and determine which programs align with your financial profile.

Moving Forward: Your Next Steps

A mortgage denial isn't the end of your homeownership journey—it's a temporary setback. By understanding why your application was denied and taking concrete steps to address those issues, you can significantly improve your chances of approval the next time around.
