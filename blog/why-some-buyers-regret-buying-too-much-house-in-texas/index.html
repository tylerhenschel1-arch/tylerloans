Why some buyers regret buying too much house in Texas

By Tyler Henschel · June 02, 2026 · Katy, TX

Why Some Buyers Regret Buying Too Much House in Texas

The Texas real estate market, particularly in and around the Houston and Katy areas, has seen explosive growth over the past decade. With competitive pricing, attractive neighborhoods, and the promise of the "Texas dream," it's easy to understand why buyers get excited about purchasing the largest home they can afford. But here's the truth: just because you *can* afford a mortgage doesn't mean you should stretch yourself to the absolute limit.

Many homebuyers in Texas end up regretting their decision to buy too much house, and understanding these regrets can help you make a smarter financial decision. As Tyler Henschel, Senior Loan Officer at CMG Home Loans in Katy, TX, often explains to clients, "The right home purchase is about more than just the monthly payment—it's about your entire financial picture and lifestyle."

The Hidden Costs Beyond the Mortgage Payment

When prospective buyers in Katy and Houston focus solely on whether they can qualify for a larger mortgage, they frequently overlook the true cost of homeownership. A massive house comes with massive expenses that extend far beyond your monthly mortgage payment.

Property Taxes and Insurance

Texas has no state income tax, which is a huge advantage—but that benefit can be offset by property taxes. In the Houston and Katy markets, property taxes are calculated based on your home's assessed value. A larger, more expensive home means higher property tax bills, sometimes significantly higher. Additionally, homeowners insurance scales with the value and size of your property. A luxury home or a sprawling estate-style property will cost considerably more to insure than a modest, well-built home.

Maintenance and Repairs

More square footage means more to maintain. Roof repairs, HVAC systems, plumbing, electrical work, and foundation issues all become exponentially more expensive on a larger property. One buyer in Katy shared that her "dream home" required a $15,000 roof repair just three years after purchase—an expense she hadn't budgeted for because she'd stretched her finances so thin on the mortgage itself.

Utilities and Energy Costs

Larger homes consume more electricity, water, and gas. In Texas summers, where air conditioning runs nearly year-round, utility bills for a sprawling home can easily exceed $300–$500 monthly—or more if the home isn't energy-efficient. Over the life of a 30-year mortgage, that's a staggering additional cost.

Landscaping and Outdoor Maintenance

Bigger lots require more upkeep. Lawn care, tree trimming, fence repairs, and pest control all add up quickly. Many buyers underestimate this expense until they're faced with replacing a sprinkler system or dealing with foundation issues caused by poor drainage on a large lot.

The Lifestyle Impact of Overextending on a Mortgage

Beyond the tangible financial costs, buying too much house can fundamentally impact your lifestyle and stress levels.

Loss of Financial Flexibility

When your mortgage consumes 40–50% of your gross monthly income (compared to the recommended 28%), you lose the ability to save, invest, or handle emergencies. A job loss, medical emergency, or market downturn becomes a genuine threat to your financial stability. You can't take that vacation, upgrade your car, or help family members in need.

Time and Energy Drains

A massive home demands your attention. Cleaning, organizing, and maintaining a 5,000-square-foot house takes time. Many buyers find themselves spending weekends on house-related tasks instead of enjoying time with family or pursuing hobbies. The home that was supposed to bring joy becomes a source of stress.

Reduced Retirement Savings

By stretching your mortgage payment to the maximum, you're sacrificing contributions to retirement accounts and investment portfolios. Tyler Henschel often advises clients to think long-term: "A larger home payment today might mean a significantly smaller retirement nest egg in 20 or 30 years. That trade-off isn't worth it for most families."

The Market Reality in Katy and Houston

The Houston and Katy markets have seen significant appreciation, and many buyers feel pressure to "buy now before prices go up further." This fear-of-missing-out mentality pushes buyers to stretch beyond their comfortable financial range. However, real estate markets are cyclical. Waiting 12 months to purchase a home that fits your budget comfortably is almost always a better decision than buying a home that stretches you thin today.

Additionally, in a market correction, buyers who overextended themselves on a larger home can find themselves underwater on their mortgage—owing more than the home is worth. This happened to thousands of Texans during the 2008 recession, and it's a risk that resurfaces whenever the market shifts.

Resale and Market Flexibility

Larger, more expensive homes are harder to sell quickly if your circumstances change. If you need to relocate for a job, downsize due to empty-nesting, or sell due to a divorce, an oversized home becomes a liability. The buyer pool for a $750,000 home is significantly smaller than the buyer pool for a $450,000 home, which means longer time on market and potential price reductions.

What's the Right Amount to Spend?

A good rule of thumb: your home payment (including property taxes, insurance, and mortgage insurance if applicable) should not exceed 28% of your gross monthly income. If you're earning $120,000 annually, that's roughly $2,800 per month for all housing costs combined. This leaves room for savings, emergency funds, and actually enjoying life outside of home maintenance.

Choosing a home that costs 15–20% less than the maximum you could borrow often results in greater long-term financial success and life satisfaction. That "smaller" home in Katy or Houston today may not have the status appeal of a luxury property, but it comes with peace of mind, financial stability, and flexibility.
