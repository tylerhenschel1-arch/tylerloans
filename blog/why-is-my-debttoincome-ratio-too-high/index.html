Why Is My Debt-to-Income Ratio Too High? A Guide for Katy and Houston Homebuyers

If you've started the mortgage application process, you've likely heard the term "debt-to-income ratio" thrown around. Maybe a lender told you yours is too high, or you're wondering what this metric even means. You're not alone. One of the most common reasons homebuyers in the Katy and Houston areas face delays—or denials—in their loan applications is an unfavorable debt-to-income ratio. Understanding what this ratio is, why it matters, and how you can address it is crucial to your path toward homeownership.

Tyler Henschel, a Senior Loan Officer at CMG Home Loans in Katy, TX, works with homebuyers every day who encounter this exact challenge. "The debt-to-income ratio is one of the first things we evaluate," Tyler explains. "It's a clear snapshot of your financial health, and lenders use it to determine whether you can comfortably manage a mortgage payment alongside your other obligations."

What Is a Debt-to-Income Ratio?

Your debt-to-income ratio (DTI) is a simple calculation: it's the percentage of your gross monthly income that goes toward debt payments. To find yours, add up all your monthly debt obligations—mortgage or rent, car loans, student loans, credit cards, personal loans, and any other recurring monthly payments—and divide that total by your gross monthly income. Then multiply by 100 to get a percentage.

Example: If your gross monthly income is $5,000 and your total monthly debt payments are $1,500, your DTI is 30%.

Most lenders, including those at CMG Home Loans, prefer to see a DTI of 43% or lower, though some loan products may have different requirements. If your ratio exceeds this threshold, your application may face obstacles.

Why Your DTI Might Be Too High

Several common situations can push your debt-to-income ratio into the red zone:

  • Student Loan Debt: Many professionals in the Houston and Katy areas carry significant student loan balances. Even if you have a strong income, substantial student loan payments can inflate your DTI quickly.
  • Credit Card Balances: Creditors calculate credit card debt based on your total credit limit, not just your current balance. A card with a $10,000 limit counts against you even if you only owe $1,000.
  • Car Loans: Multiple vehicle loans or recent auto purchases can add hundreds of dollars to your monthly obligations.
  • Personal Loans: Any outstanding personal loans factor into your calculation.
  • Alimony or Child Support: These mandatory payments are included in your DTI.
  • New Mortgage Payment: Lenders factor in the projected mortgage payment for the home you're trying to buy, which can push your ratio over the limit if your other debts are already substantial.
  • Lower Income: A temporary job loss, reduced hours, or recent career change can lower your income and push your ratio upward.

How Lenders Use Your DTI

Tyler explains that lenders view DTI as a risk indicator. "A high ratio suggests you're stretched thin financially. If an unexpected expense comes up, you might struggle to make your mortgage payment." From a lender's perspective, a borrower with a 50% DTI is riskier than one with a 30% DTI.

Different loan programs may have varying DTI requirements. Conventional loans often cap DTI at 43%, while some government-backed programs may allow higher ratios under specific circumstances. The exact threshold depends on your credit score, down payment amount, savings, and the lender's underwriting guidelines.

What You Can Do If Your DTI Is Too High

If you've discovered your ratio exceeds acceptable limits, don't lose hope. Several strategies can help improve your situation:

Pay Down Existing Debt

The most direct approach is to reduce your outstanding debt obligations. Focus on credit cards and personal loans first, as these typically carry higher interest rates. Paying down a credit card balance not only lowers your monthly payment but may also lower your overall credit utilization ratio, which can boost your credit score.

Increase Your Income

If you've recently received a raise, taken on a second job, or started a side business, you might be able to use that additional income in your application. However, most lenders require documentation that the income is stable and likely to continue. A recent promotion, for example, might need to show a two-year history with that employer.

Extend Loan Terms

If you have outstanding loans with substantial monthly payments, exploring options to extend the repayment term might lower your monthly obligations. This won't eliminate the debt but can improve your DTI temporarily. Keep in mind that extending a term typically means paying more interest over time.

Look for a Co-Borrower

Adding a co-borrower with strong income and low debt can improve the overall application ratio. This might be a spouse, parent, or trusted family member willing to take on the mortgage responsibility with you.

Delay Your Home Purchase

While not the answer anyone wants to hear, sometimes the best strategy is to wait a few months while you aggressively pay down debt. This gives you time to improve your financial profile and enter the homebuying process in a stronger position.

Consider Different Loan Products

Some loan programs have more flexible DTI requirements than others. Speaking with an experienced loan officer can help you explore all available options for your specific situation.
