Why is my debt-to-income ratio too high

By Tyler Henschel · June 01, 2026 · Katy, TX

Why Is My Debt-to-Income Ratio Too High? A Guide to Understanding and Improving Yours

If you've recently applied for a mortgage in the Katy or Houston area, you may have received disappointing news: your debt-to-income ratio is too high. This is one of the most common reasons lenders deny mortgage applications, and it can feel frustrating when you're ready to buy your dream home.

But here's the good news—understanding why your debt-to-income ratio matters and what causes it to be high is the first step toward improving your financial profile. In this guide, we'll walk you through the details of debt-to-income ratios, explain why lenders care so much about this number, and discuss what you can do to strengthen your application.

What Is a Debt-to-Income Ratio?

Your debt-to-income ratio (often abbreviated as DTI) is a simple calculation that shows how much of your gross monthly income goes toward paying debts. Lenders use this metric to assess whether you can comfortably afford a new mortgage payment on top of your existing obligations.

The formula is straightforward:

Total Monthly Debt Payments ÷ Gross Monthly Income = Debt-to-Income Ratio

For example, if you earn $5,000 gross per month and your total monthly debt payments are $1,500, your DTI would be 30 percent.

Why Do Lenders Focus on DTI?

Lenders view your DTI ratio as a clear indicator of your ability to take on new debt responsibly. The lower your ratio, the more financial breathing room you have, and the less risky you appear as a borrower. Conversely, a high DTI suggests that a significant portion of your income is already committed to existing debts, leaving less room for a mortgage payment.

What Counts as Debt in Your DTI Calculation?

Not all financial obligations count toward your DTI, but many do. Understanding what's included can help you see where your number might be higher than you'd like.

Debts Typically Included in DTI Calculations:

  • Auto loan payments
  • Student loan payments
  • Credit card payments (typically calculated as a percentage of your total balance, not just your minimum payment)
  • Personal loans
  • Medical debt payments
  • Child support or alimony obligations
  • Current mortgage or rent payments (if applicable)
  • Home equity line of credit (HELOC) payments
  • The anticipated new mortgage payment

What Usually Doesn't Count:

  • Utility bills and insurance premiums (in most cases)
  • Groceries and gas
  • Cell phone bills
  • Subscription services

What DTI Ratio Do Lenders Prefer?

Most traditional lenders prefer to see a DTI ratio below 43 percent, though some standard purchase loan programs may allow higher ratios with automated underwriting approval and strong compensating factors. Specialty programs — such as construction loans — often have stricter caps (for example, CMG's conventional construction program caps back-end DTI at 45 percent and front-end at 38 percent). Your loan officer can tell you exactly which DTI ceiling applies to the specific program you're pursuing.

If you are a business owner, self-employed, or a real estate investor and your DTI is pushing past the conforming limits, it is worth knowing that CMG's Non-QM programs (Edge, Sharp, and Advantage series) can accommodate DTIs up to 55 percent in many scenarios. That is a meaningful difference for borrowers whose tax-return income understates their actual cash flow, which is common across the Houston business community.

FHA loans are also worth a look if your DTI is above the conventional preference. FHA loans run through the FHA TOTAL Scorecard (automated underwriting), which routinely approves DTIs above 43 percent and can go higher depending on the rest of the file — the AUS approves what it approves, so the rigid "43 percent ceiling" framing does not really apply the same way to FHA financing.

Some lenders may use two separate calculations: a front-end ratio (also called housing ratio) that includes only your new mortgage payment divided by gross income, and a back-end ratio that includes all debts. Your back-end ratio is typically the more stringent measure.

Common Reasons Your Debt-to-Income Ratio Is Too High

1. High Credit Card Balances

Credit card debt is one of the biggest culprits behind elevated DTI ratios. Even if you only make minimum payments, lenders calculate your credit card obligation as a percentage of your total credit card balance, which can significantly impact your ratio. If you have multiple cards with high balances, this alone could push your DTI above acceptable thresholds.

2. Student Loan Debt

Many homebuyers in the Houston and Katy area are carrying substantial student loan balances. These payments are always included in DTI calculations, and they can substantially increase your ratio, particularly if you're on an income-driven repayment plan.

3. Auto Loans

Whether you're making payments on one vehicle or two, auto loans count fully toward your DTI. Recent vehicle purchases or financing multiple cars can quickly push your ratio higher.

4. Personal Loans

Personal loans and other installment debt are fully factored into lender calculations. If you've borrowed money for home improvements, debt consolidation, or other purposes, these payments will increase your DTI.

5. Insufficient Income

Sometimes the issue isn't too much debt—it's not enough income. If your gross monthly income is lower than it needs to be relative to your debts, your DTI will be high regardless of how responsible you've been.

6. The Anticipated Mortgage Payment

Remember, lenders calculate your DTI including the new mortgage payment you're planning to take on. If you're looking at homes in a higher price range, the mortgage payment alone might push you over acceptable limits.

Steps to Lower Your Debt-to-Income Ratio

Pay Down High-Balance Debts

Focusing extra payments on credit cards and other unsecured debt can reduce your DTI more quickly than paying down long-term installment loans. Even paying off a single high-balance credit card can make a meaningful difference.

Increase Your Income

If your income can be documented, increases through a second job, raise, or bonus can improve your ratio without requiring you to reduce spending.

Delay Your Mortgage Application

If you're in a position to wait, giving yourself time to pay down debt and build additional income can strengthen your application significantly.

Look at Your Target Home Price

Consider whether you might qualify for a home at a slightly lower price point. This reduces the anticipated mortgage payment and can bring your DTI within acceptable range.
