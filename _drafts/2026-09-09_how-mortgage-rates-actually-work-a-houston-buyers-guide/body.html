How Mortgage Rates Actually Work — A Houston Buyer's Guide

If you're shopping for a home anywhere in the Greater Houston area—whether in Pearland, The Woodlands, Cypress, Sugar Land, or closer to downtown—you've probably noticed that mortgage rates seem to change constantly. One week your lender quotes you one number, the next week it's different. You might even wonder: why does my neighbor get a different rate than me?

The truth is, mortgage rates aren't one-size-fits-all, and they're definitely not pulled out of thin air. Understanding how rates work will help you navigate the home-buying process with more confidence and make smarter financial decisions. Let's break it down.

The Big Picture: What Drives Mortgage Rates

Mortgage rates don't exist in a vacuum. They're influenced by broader economic forces, and the primary factor is the bond market—specifically, the 10-year Treasury yield. When Treasury yields rise, mortgage rates tend to rise. When they fall, mortgage rates often follow suit.

Think of it this way: investors can buy U.S. Treasuries, which are considered very safe, or they can buy mortgage-backed securities (MBS), which carry more risk. When Treasury yields are attractive, investors may demand higher returns on mortgages to compensate for the added risk. This pushes mortgage rates up. Conversely, when Treasuries offer less appeal, investors become more willing to accept lower returns on mortgages, which can push rates down.

Other macroeconomic factors—inflation, employment data, Federal Reserve policy, and even global events—ripple through the bond market and ultimately affect what you pay for your home loan.

Why Your Rate Is Unique to You

While the broader market sets a baseline, your individual rate depends on several personal and loan factors. This is where things get more granular, and it's important to understand that two buyers closing on the same day can legitimately receive different rates.

Credit Score

Your credit score is one of the most significant factors lenders use to price your loan. A higher credit score generally signals lower risk to a lender, which can translate into a more favorable rate. Conversely, a lower score may result in a higher rate to compensate the lender for increased risk.

Loan-to-Value (LTV) Ratio

How much you're borrowing relative to the home's value matters. If you're putting down 20% and borrowing 80%, you'll typically receive better pricing than someone putting down 5% and borrowing 95%. The larger your down payment, the lower the risk to the lender, and the more competitive your rate can be.

Loan Type

Are you pursuing a conventional loan, an FHA loan, a VA loan, or a USDA loan? Each product has its own risk profile and market pricing. Conventional loans often carry different rates than government-backed options, depending on the broader lending environment.

Loan Term and Structure

A 30-year mortgage will typically carry a different rate than a 15-year mortgage. Shorter terms often have lower rates because the lender's risk window is smaller. Your choice of loan structure—whether you're interested in an adjustable-rate product or a traditional 30-year amortization—also influences pricing.

Property Type and Location

Whether you're buying a single-family home in Memorial, a townhouse in Heights, or a new construction in Tomball, the property type can affect your rate. Primary residences usually receive better pricing than investment properties. The property's condition, age, and location within the Houston metro also play a role in how lenders assess risk.

Debt-to-Income Ratio (DTI)

Lenders look at your total monthly debt payments (mortgage, auto loans, student loans, credit cards, etc.) relative to your gross monthly income. A lower DTI is generally viewed as less risky and may result in more competitive pricing. Different loan programs accommodate different DTI thresholds, which can also affect the rate you're offered.

Down Payment and Savings

Lenders also consider whether you have cash reserves after closing. Borrowers with more savings in the bank are viewed as lower-risk, which can influence your rate.

Rate, Points, and Closing Costs: The Trade-Offs

One concept that confuses many buyers is the relationship between your mortgage rate and the fees you pay upfront. When a lender quotes you a rate, they're also quoting you a loan structure that includes various costs.

In some cases, you can "buy down" your rate by paying points at closing—essentially paying more upfront to receive a lower rate. In other scenarios, you might accept a slightly higher rate in exchange for the lender covering more of your closing costs. Neither option is universally better; it depends on your financial situation, how long you plan to stay in the home, and your cash position at closing.

Lock-In: Protecting Your Rate

Once you and your lender agree on a rate, you can choose to "lock" it in. A rate lock protects you from further rate increases during your loan processing and underwriting period—typically 15, 30, 45, or 60 days, depending on what you negotiate.

If rates rise during your lock period, your rate stays the same. If rates fall, however, you're generally stuck with your locked rate (though some lenders offer limited "float-down" provisions as an added feature).

Rate locks are crucial in a volatile market. If you're in the early stages of shopping in neighborhoods like Friendswood, Magnolia, or Conroe, discussing your lock strategy with your lender should be part of your overall home-buying plan.

What You Can Control

While you can't control the broader economy or bond markets, you do have leverage over several factors that affect your rate:

  • Improve your credit score. Pay bills on time, reduce outstanding debt, and avoid opening new credit accounts right before applying for a mortgage.
  • Save for a larger down payment. The more equity you bring to the table, the stronger your position for rate pricing.
  • Reduce your debt-to-income ratio. Pay down existing debts or increase your income before applying, if possible.
  • Shop around. Different lenders price loans differently. Getting quotes from multiple lenders can reveal meaningful differences in how they structure rates and costs.
  • Choose the right loan product. Conventional, FHA, VA, and USDA loans all have different pricing models. Your lender can help you determine which product aligns best with your financial profile.
  • Build reserves. Showing lenders that you have savings after closing reduces their risk perception and can improve your pricing.
